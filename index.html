The psychology behind the game
  • Adaptive difficulty. Each room aims for about 75% success. Too easy and nothing changes; too hard and you give up. This is the “zone of proximal development”.
  • Retrieval practice. Pulling an answer from memory strengthens it more than rereading. Every quiz explains its answer right after you commit.
  • Spaced repetition. Words you struggle with come back a few sessions later, just as you're about to forget them.
  • Interleaving. The Daily Mix switches topics on purpose. It feels harder, and that is why it sticks.
  • Dual coding. The color wheel, fabric layouts and floor plans pair pictures with ideas, so you have two routes back to each idea.
  • Growth mindset. Feedback praises strategy and effort. A drop in difficulty is a reset, not a verdict.
  • Stories and places. Facts tied to a dish, an outfit and a spot on the map are far easier to recall than isolated dates. This is elaborative encoding, and memory athletes use the same “memory palace” idea.
  • A daily ritual. The World Tour is short, shared and once a day. Small, regular sessions beat cramming, and comparing with friends adds the social motivation that keeps habits going.
  • Real conflict research. Peacemaker draws on Gottman's marriage studies, Fisher & Ury's Getting to Yes, Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication and Lieberman's affect-labeling experiments.
